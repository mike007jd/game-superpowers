# Gameplay

## Make the action matter

Tie the primary action to a readable consequence and a reason to act again. Let the player understand what they control, what can change, and how their decisions affect it. Choose challenge, discovery, expression or other motivations that fit the intended experience; failure and progression are design choices.

When a mechanic is uncertain, build a repeatable situation that exposes the question. Preserve enough of the intended camera, controls and feedback to judge it. Expand a successful interaction into the requested game rather than accumulating disconnected demonstrations.

## Give choices consequences

Different options should change decisions or outcomes when they promise gameplay variety. Make their trade-offs legible in the situations where they matter. Cosmetic variation can serve identity without changing the rules.

Add content around missing experiences: a new demand, a useful alternative, a discovery or a payoff. Tune pacing through changes in pressure and recovery. Judge repetition by whether it supports mastery or enjoyment, and give progression an opportunity to be felt in play.

## Keep the rules coherent

Make visible contact, legal actions, costs and rewards agree with the game state. When failure belongs in the game, communicate its cause and provide a useful way to continue or try again. Choose forgiveness and timing for the action instead of importing another game's settings.

Define which simulation, presentation and real-world clocks advance during pause or interruption. Keep held input, temporary effects and progress consistent when play resumes. Use the project's existing state and timing model unless it causes the problem being solved.
