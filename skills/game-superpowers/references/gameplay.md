# Gameplay

## Make the action matter

Tie the primary action to a readable consequence and a reason to act again. The player should understand what they control, what can change and how their decisions affect it. Choose the motivation that fits the intended experience, such as challenge, discovery or expression. Decide deliberately whether failure and progression exist and what form they take.

When a mechanic is uncertain, build a repeatable test situation that exposes the question, with enough of the intended camera, controls and feedback to judge it. Grow a successful interaction into the requested game instead of piling up disconnected demos.

Common faults:
- The core action has no consequence the player can see within about a second.
- The goal or fail condition exists in code but is never shown.
- The first minute contains no decision, only waiting or reading.

## Give choices consequences

Options that promise gameplay variety must change decisions or outcomes. Make their trade-offs legible in the situations where they matter. Cosmetic variation can serve identity without changing the rules.

Add content to fill a missing experience: a new demand, a useful alternative, a discovery or a payoff. Do not add content just to fill space. Pace the game by alternating pressure and recovery. Repetition is fine when it builds mastery or is enjoyable in itself. Let progression show up in play, not only in numbers.

## Tune difficulty

Expose tuning values (speed, damage, spawn rate, timers) as named constants or data rather than scattered literals. Start with a forgiving first encounter and raise one variable at a time. Borrowed numbers can serve as a starting point, but tune them to this game's camera, scale and input. A difficulty spike should come from new demands, not hidden rules or unreadable hazards.

## Keep the rules coherent

Visible contact, legal actions, costs and rewards must agree with the game state. Hitboxes should match what the player sees and lean in the player's favor. When failure belongs in the game, show its cause and offer a fast way to retry or continue.

Decide which clocks keep running during pause or interruption: simulation, presentation and real-world time. Held input, temporary effects and progress must stay consistent when play resumes. Use the project's existing state and timing model unless that model causes the problem being solved.
